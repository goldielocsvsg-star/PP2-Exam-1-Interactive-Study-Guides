💊 Calcium Channel Blockers

Your Complete CCB Study Guide

🎯 The Big Picture

What Do CCBs Do?

Calcium Channel Blockers prevent calcium from entering cells of the heart and blood vessels, leading to vasodilation and effects on cardiac conduction.

There are two main types of CCBs with very different clinical effects:

🔵 Dihydropyridines (DHPs) 🟣 Non-Dihydropyridines

💡 Memory Trick!

DHP drugs end in "-dipine" (Nifedipine, Amlodipine, Felodipine)
Non-DHPs: Verapamil & Diltiazem (the "odd ones out" that affect the heart more!)

⚖️ DHP vs Non-DHP: Know the Difference!

🔵 Dihydropyridines

Nifedipine, Amlodipine, Felodipine

  • Act primarily on peripheral arterioles
  • Cause vasodilation → ↓ SVR → ↓ BP
  • Minimal direct cardiac suppression
  • More likely to cause reflex tachycardia
  • Does NOT slow heart rate
📍 Primary Target: BLOOD VESSELS

🟣 Non-Dihydropyridines

Verapamil, Diltiazem

  • Act on both arterioles AND heart
  • ↓ Heart rate
  • ↓ AV conduction
  • ↓ Myocardial contractility
  • Useful for dysrhythmias (AV nodal suppression)
📍 Targets: BLOOD VESSELS + HEART
Heart Rate

↑ or unchanged (reflex tachycardia)

Heart Rate

↓ Slowed (bradycardia risk)

AV Conduction

No significant effect

AV Conduction

↓ Slowed (AV block risk)

⚡ Reflex Tachycardia (Important!)

⚠️ What Happens: Rapid vasodilation (especially with IR nifedipine) activates the baroreceptor reflex → increased sympathetic activity → ↑ Heart rate + ↑ Contractility

How to minimize reflex effects:

💊

Extended-Release

Use ER formulations instead of immediate-release to prevent rapid BP drops

🤝

Add Beta-Blocker

Concurrent beta-blocker therapy with DHPs blocks reflex tachycardia

🚫

Avoid IR Nifedipine

IR nifedipine causes most reflex tachycardia - use sustained-release instead

🚨 SAFETY ALERT: Immediate-Release Nifedipine

⛔ BLACK BOX WARNING TERRITORY!

Immediate-release nifedipine has been associated with:
  • Increased mortality in MI and unstable angina
  • Increased risk of myocardial infarction in hypertension
✅ Safe alternatives: Sustained-release nifedipine and other long-acting CCBs do NOT share this risk!

📌 Bottom Line

IR nifedipine should be avoided or used with extreme caution. Always prefer extended-release formulations!

❤️ Cardiac Safety Considerations

🚫 Verapamil & Diltiazem Contraindications

  • Sick sinus syndrome (without pacemaker)
  • Second-degree AV block
  • Third-degree AV block
  • Severe bradycardia
  • Decompensated heart failure

✅ Dihydropyridines Preferred When

  • Patient has AV block
  • Patient has bradycardia
  • Patient has heart failure
  • Patient needs BP control without cardiac suppression
💡 Why DHPs are safer for cardiac patients: They lack significant cardiac suppression - they work primarily on blood vessels!

⚠️ Common Adverse Effects (Class-Wide)

Most adverse effects are related to vasodilation:

🤕

Headache

From vasodilation of cerebral vessels

😵

Dizziness

Due to BP reduction

😊

Facial Flushing

Vasodilation of facial vessels

🦶

Peripheral Edema

Ankles & feet swelling

🦷

Gingival Hyperplasia

Gum overgrowth with long-term use

👴

Skin Eruptions

Eczematous rash in older adults

💡 Remember "HEDFE" for Vasodilation Effects

Headache, Edema, Dizziness, Flushing, Eczema (in elderly)

💊 Drug & Food Interactions

Interacting Agent Effect Clinical Significance
Beta Blockers + Verapamil/Diltiazem ⚠️ Severe bradycardia, AV block, HF DANGEROUS combination!
Beta Blockers + DHPs ✅ Beneficial - reduces reflex tachycardia Often used together intentionally
Digoxin + Verapamil ↑ Digoxin levels ~60% Combined AV suppression → heart block risk
🍊 Grapefruit Juice ↑ CCB serum levels Especially nifedipine & felodipine
🍊 Grapefruit Juice Note: Risk is highest with nifedipine & felodipine, but still possible with verapamil, diltiazem, and amlodipine. Advise patients accordingly!

☠️ Toxicity & Overdose Management

⚠️ At toxic doses: Loss of vascular selectivity → cardiac suppression even with DHPs!

Manifestations: Severe hypotension, bradycardia, AV block

Treatment Steps:

1
Activated Charcoal - Decontamination if recent ingestion
2
IV Calcium Gluconate - Improves contractility and vasodilation (ANTIDOTE!)
3
Atropine - For bradycardia
4
Vasopressors - (e.g., norepinephrine) for hypotension
5
Temporary Pacing - If needed for severe bradycardia/block

🧠 Toxicity Antidote Memory Tip

"Calcium blocks Calcium blockers!" - IV Calcium Gluconate is the key antidote!

👥 Special Populations

🤰 Pregnancy & Lactation

  • CCBs cross the placenta
  • Animal studies suggest potential fetal harm
  • Use only if benefits outweigh risks
  • Verapamil may be excreted in breast milk
  • Nifedipine: insufficient breastfeeding data - use caution

👶 Infants & Children

  • Verapamil used for selected dysrhythmias
  • CCBs used for pediatric HTN
  • CCBs used for cardiomyopathy

👴 Older Adults

  • Higher risk for constipation (verapamil)
  • Increased susceptibility to edema
  • More prone to skin reactions
  • Start low, go slow!

🫀 Hepatic Impairment

  • Verapamil requires dose reduction
  • Diltiazem requires dose reduction
  • Both metabolized hepatically

👩‍⚕️ Key Nursing Monitoring Points

📊 Monitor

  • Blood pressure
  • Heart rate
  • ECG (for non-DHPs)
  • Signs of edema
  • Signs of heart failure
  • Bowel function (verapamil → constipation)

📚 Patient Teaching

  • Do NOT crush ER formulations!
  • Report dizziness immediately
  • Report swelling (ankles/feet)
  • Report slow pulse
  • Avoid grapefruit juice if advised
  • Rise slowly (orthostatic hypotension)

✅ Evaluate Response

  • Reduced blood pressure
  • Decreased angina frequency
  • Controlled ventricular rate (dysrhythmias)
  • No adverse effects
